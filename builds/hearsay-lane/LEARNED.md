# Hearsay Lane, a neighbourhood relationship toy

Notes from the Saxton Showcase, written for an AI helper as much as for a person. Hand this file to your agent along with `hearsay-lane.html`, the build itself, and it gets what we learned without having to rebuild it to find out.

- Built: 2026-09-23
- Tags: game, life sim, toy, simulation, canvas, touch, keyboard, web audio
- Who made it: Amy, our AI helper (an AI, not a person), built it because we asked for it. People decided it should be made and approved putting it on the shelf. In the notes below, "I" is Amy, and a "session" is one of her working runs.
- The build: one self-contained HTML file. Open it in a current browser; it needs no install, no account and no internet. It makes sound with Web Audio, which the browser starts only after your first tap, click or key. Anything it saves (progress, scores or settings) stays in your own browser's localStorage and is never sent anywhere.

## What it is

A single-file canvas toy. A street of 6 to 8 houses and 10 to 14 residents seen from above, each resident with two or three made-up leanings (chatty, grumpy, romantic, gardener, night owl, early bird, bookworm, sporty, tidy, messy, foodie, musical). Days pass in three phases. Residents visit each other or go to the park or the corner shop, and every meeting moves a friendship score by how their leanings get along plus seeded luck. Pairs move through nine states (strangers, acquaintances, friends, best friends, rivals, crush, dating, married, exes), and each change lands in a story feed as a short cheerful line. Lines between people glow green for friends, red for rivals and pink for romance, thicker when stronger, and flash when the pair meets. The player nudges by throwing a party at a house with chosen guests, introducing two people, starting a rumour that one fancies the other (it can backfire and someone gets the blame), or planting a community garden that draws the gardeners together. Tapping a resident opens a panel to rename them, change their leanings and see their relationships. Weddings move one partner in with the other, and an empty house gets a newcomer. The street autosaves in localStorage and exports or imports as .json. Pause, 1x, 2x, 3x, with Space to pause and auto-pause on blur. ?preview=1 runs the street at 3x with a two-line feed drawn on the canvas.

## What we learned building it

Putting every rule in one pure block that owns its own seeded random state (the mulberry32 state lives inside the saved street) made three things cheap: node could run 40 streets for 90 days in a couple of seconds, a street saved mid-run and reloaded continues exactly as if it had never been saved, and tuning became measurement. The first rule set produced about 2 weddings and zero break-ups per street in 60 days; couples gained romance faster than it decayed. Scaling couples' romance gain down, giving dating (not married) couples a faster decay and more tiffs when their leanings clash brought it to roughly one wedding and one break-up per two streets, with rumours as the player's lever for more. Keeping the feed honest was easier as a data rule than as prose: state lines are keyed by from>to transition, so a line can only ever describe the transition that just happened, and a test reads every rendered line for state words.

## The gotcha

Two of my own errors, both caught by checking. First, the desktop introduce check failed although the feature worked: the street keeps running at 3x while the test clicks, so ten or more new lines could land after the intro line and push it out of the 'last six events' window I was checking. The nudge checks now look for an event of the right kind with an id newer than the one taken just before the click. Second, I wrote transition and feed-line counts into the header comment before measuring them, and they were wrong (I had guessed about 4,700 and 98,000; the suite checks 5,823 transitions and 19,482 feed lines). The suite now prints those counts on every run and the header quotes them. A smaller one: a variable named simSpeed matched a case-insensitive grep for the genre's trademark, so it was renamed.
